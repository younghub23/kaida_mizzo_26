Throughout the earliest years of my life, I was a young girl whose knowledge about the world was limited to the relationships around me and how I fit into those dynamics, making the people around me my entire world. I was a single child for approximately seventy-five unrecountable minutes before my twin sister Chiara followed me out of the womb. For being twins, we look incredibly different: I look like my dad while she looks like my mom; where my hair is nearly black, hers is a golden shade of brown; and for a large majority of my life, whenever we were found in public with one of our sisters, our twinship would be looked over in favor of a stronger physical resemblance between her and my other sisters. However, regardless of our lacking physical similarity and the hour that we were separated at birth, Chiara is the first person I ever knew and the first person who made up my world.

Our relationship and growing up as a twin has been one of the most important forces in shaping how I've grown up and my identity altogether. Chiara and I were inseparable up until middle school – playing for the same soccer club, going to the same school with the same friends, and doing the same activities – it was nice to have a "built-in best friend" but also incredibly challenging to have a "mirror," and it was something that I really struggled with as I grew up. Naturally, as two twins who do the same activities, there will always be comparison that exists between us. We go to the same school, so which one of us takes the better classes and has the better grades? We play the same sport, so which one of us is on a better team? For a long time I measured myself entirely against her, and because we shared every category of my life, every category became a place I could come up short – less athletic, less fit, less smart, less liked, less pretty. It took me years to understand that a mirror was never meant to tell me I was falling behind; it was only ever showing me a version of myself that I had the power to change.

Two years after us, my younger sister Gia was born, and in the following three years after her birth came my brother Christian and eventually my youngest sister Gaby. Being the eldest daughter of five taught me responsibility before I really understood what the word meant. Because I was the oldest, my parents expected a lot from me, and it was hard to be five years old and getting in trouble for not setting the dishes when Chiara – the same age as me, born the same hour – wasn't facing the same anger. I learned to be independent early, not because I chose to, but because there was always another baby who needed my parents more than I did, and so by the time I was little I was already used to facing situations on my own. Later, watching my younger siblings gain access to resources I only received much later, and succeeding at ages where I was still figuring things out, was difficult in a way I didn't always know how to name. But it taught me one of the most important lessons I carry today: how to separate my own life, my own achievements, and my own path from the achievements of the people around me, and how to be genuinely, uncomplicatedly happy for them.

Growing up in the Potomac bubble, I hadn't realized how disconnected I felt from my own culture until I was suddenly surrounded by people who shared it – teammates from completely different backgrounds and places, so unlike the world I'd grown up in, who became some of the people I love most. Through that team I understood parts of my identity I hadn't known were missing, and I started accepting pieces of myself I hadn't even realized I'd learned to quiet. For someone who had spent years feeling like the "less than" version of her twin, standing on that field was the first time I fully understood that my worth was not something I had to win in comparison to anyone else.

Soccer taught me the lessons I still build my life around. It showed me the value of hard work – that if you put the work in, the work will show. It taught me that it is up to me, and up to God, to change the circumstances of my life; that what you're not changing, you are choosing; and that peace comes from focusing only on what you can actually control. Most of all, it taught me what it is to pursue something with everything you have and to keep meeting obstacle after obstacle, and to keep going anyway. Someone close to me once told me, "For so long soccer was everything to you because it was – it was all that you guys knew. But now you are at a point where you realize there is so much more to the world around you, and there are so many possibilities for you that you didn't realize existed before." That sentence has stayed with me, because it marks the moment I stopped letting one thing define my entire worth and started to see how much wider my life could be.

Track became the first place I tested that wider version of myself. For most of my life I had done things because Chiara did them too, or because my family expected it; track was the first thing I made entirely my own – and, at first, something I genuinely struggled at. There is a specific kind of pain in running: the moment when you've already given everything you have and still have to reach down and find something more. Track taught me to convert that pain into discipline, and to understand that life is going to bring struggle no matter what, so you might as well choose the struggle – choose challenge, actively, every single day. It stripped away my limiting beliefs; I learned that there is no goal I can set for myself that isn't achievable with enough work and effort, and that hard work leads directly, visibly, to results.

The biggest part of that was my coach, CJ. For the first time in my life, I experienced a coach who actually believed in me – which, after so many years of feeling overlooked, felt like an answered prayer. Track also demanded a new kind of independence, because it was something I pursued largely on my own – learning how to push through and take care of myself and stay disciplined even when my family couldn't always support me in it. In choosing that challenge every day, I became someone who knows how to push through difficulty rather than run from it.

Underneath all of it, quietly at first and then completely, is my relationship with God. I grew up in a very Catholic family, but for most of my life I didn't really understand Catholicism – or Christianity at all. I didn't have my own relationship with God; I felt forced to go to church every week, and while I watched my friends grow deeply involved in their faith and quietly wanted that for myself, I never resonated with it or understood it. That began to change this past winter, in the middle of a season of real anxiety about the uncertainty of my future – most of it, honestly, about soccer. I started reading Bible plans on my phone and slowly began to see how the word of God actually applied to my life, how people use it to navigate the way they walk through the world.

Then came the complications with the Asia Cup. There were endless plans and, for a while, my parents almost didn't let us go – which was heartbreaking, because it was something I had worked toward and thought about every single day since I first learned it was possible. Right before Lent, I found myself completely out of control of my own future: it came down to my parents' decision, then Selu's, then Naz's, and I finally recognized that only God could change their minds. So I begged Him to make a way. I read the Bible every day of Lent and gave up cursing – the first year I truly tried to give something up for God rather than for myself – and I felt genuinely drawn to His word. Somehow, we were able to go. I played all of every game (minus four minutes of stoppage time in the first) and had the most unreal experience of my life. Now, when I struggle or feel that same anxiety, I try to look to God first and trust that I am walking in the plans He has for me. Proverbs 3:5–6 has become the verse I hold onto: "Trust in the Lord with all your heart and lean not on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge Him, and He shall make your paths straight." My faith is what keeps me disciplined in the face of uncertainty, and it is the lens through which I now understand everything else.

When I look back at the young girl whose entire world was the people around her, I don't think she was wrong – just early. The people around me really did shape me: Chiara taught me who I am by showing me who I'm not; my siblings taught me responsibility and how to hold my own path apart from theirs; soccer and the Philippines gave me my confidence and my culture; track gave me something that was mine; and God gave me a way to make sense of all of it. I am still, in many ways, that girl trying to understand where she fits – but now I understand that fitting was never the point. The point was becoming someone worth being, and trusting that the path I'm walking, comparison and struggle and all, is straightening out exactly the way it's meant to.
